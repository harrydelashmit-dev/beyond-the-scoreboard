**Ten main slides · 20 minutes · two slides and four minutes per member**

Stronger football is linked to higher ticket receipts, but revenue growth falls short of spending growth in our main models. Prior support dependence separates programs with stronger and weaker recovery.

## Slide 1 — Most athletics departments need additional funding

**0:00–1:45 · Evan West**

Open with GAO’s 94% statistic for whole Division I athletics departments in 2023–2024. This is external context, not a result from our 2010–2019 panel or a football-only deficit statistic. Ask why spending keeps growing when so many departments need institutional support. A funding gap before allocations is different from a deficit after support.


## Slide 2 — Does the extra money keep up with the extra cost?

**1:45–4:00 · Evan West**

State the financial question plainly. A stronger season can accompany more receipts, yet those receipts may not match growing expenses. Winning benefits and returns to additional spending are separate questions. Preview the path: tickets, cost recovery, funding model, actual support burden. Keep hype, reputation and campus-pride arguments out of the evidence story because we did not measure them.


## Slide 3 — We compared universities with their own earlier years

**4:00–5:30 · Luis Ramos**

Use a simple 2010–2019 timeline and the source names, not a methods lecture. Knight-Newhouse supplies finances, ESPN football strength, IPEDS university size and students, BLS inflation. Validated school/year joins and within-school comparisons reduce confusion between large and small institutions. Samples vary: the ticket result uses 817 school-years/109 universities; annual recovery uses 2,002/227. These are observational comparisons.


## Slide 4 — Stronger football is linked to more ticket money

**5:30–8:00 · Luis Ramos**

Explain the dot and uncertainty line once. A ten-point increase in ESPN FPI is associated with 6.41% higher following-year all-athletics ticket receipts; the 95% range is 3.99%–8.89%. N=817, 109 schools. The association survives its related-test correction. FPI points are not wins, and the receipts are not football-only tickets or attendance. Price and capacity can matter. Handoff: money comes in, but how much does spending grow alongside it?

## Slide 5 — Each extra spending dollar accompanies about 64 cents of revenue

**8:00–10:00 · Harrison Delashmit**

Harrison presents the central result. Annual changes give 0.64 revenue dollars per additional spending dollar, with a 95% range of 0.50–0.79, N=2,002/227 schools. The test is full coverage, slope equals one, and remains below that benchmark after adjustment. The three-year accumulated-flow estimate is 0.68, range 0.55–0.81; it supports the same main comparison without needing another slide. Revenue removes institutional/government allocations and student fees from exported totals.

Chart: [source PNG](../results/presentation_charts/07_cost_recovery.png)


## Slide 6 — The remaining gap needs a funding explanation

**10:00–12:00 · Harrison Delashmit**

Clarify the meaning of 64 cents. It is a budget association, not a randomized return, a guaranteed loss or an immediate 36-cent taxpayer bill. Revenue can fund spending; transfers, restricted/in-kind gifts and accounting definitions complicate a cash interpretation. The three-year result concerns accumulated budget flows, not a one-time investment. Briefly disclose the contrary levels comparison: 0.93, 95% range 0.82–1.04, which includes one. The conclusion is strongest for the main change models. Handoff: the average hides important differences in how programs are funded.


## Slide 7 — Prior support dependence separates cost recovery

**12:00–14:00 · Robert Philyaw**

Compare 0.70 dollars of recovery for lower-support programs with 0.30 for higher-support programs. High dependence means allocations/student fees covered at least 50% of expenses in 2010–2012; outcomes are evaluated later. The direct difference is -0.395, interval -0.588 to -0.203, family-adjusted p=0.000214; it also survives all-fourteen adjustment. N=1,521/219 schools. These are funding classifications, not judgments of educational value. Avoid claiming every small or non-Power-Five program has the same result.

Chart: [source PNG](../results/presentation_charts/08_support_groups.png)


## Slide 8 — A smaller percentage can hide a larger dollar commitment

**14:00–16:00 · Robert Philyaw**

Use the same 222 universities at both endpoints. Inflation-adjusted allocated support plus student fees rose from about $2.34 billion to $3.09 billion, an increase of 31.7%, while support/expenses fell from 31.2% to 28.0%. This is a descriptive matched-school accounting comparison, not a regression or an effect of winning. Explain that expenses grew enough to make support a smaller slice while actual support increased. Handoff: the CFO needs to watch dollars, not just ratios.

Chart: [source PNG](../results/presentation_charts/09_support_dollars_share.png)


## Slide 9 — Ask what additional funding the university must commit

**16:00–18:00 · Morgan Beckler**

Turn the evidence into three practical questions: what incremental receipts can we document, which funding-model peers provide a sensible benchmark, and what continuing university support is required? Track actual dollars as well as percentages. Do not invent tuition, brand values or hypothetical repayment schedules. This is an evaluation framework, not a recommendation to cut every program or a prediction for a specific school.


## Slide 10 — More revenue does not establish financial self-sufficiency

**18:00–20:00 · Morgan Beckler**

Close with the supported connections: stronger football and ticket receipts; incomplete recovery in the main budget-change models; weaker recovery among previously support-dependent programs; and rising support dollars despite a lower expense share. Give a short disclosure: recruitment, spending-success and breakthrough pathways were also tested but did not give consistent primary evidence. Detailed results are in backup. Historical associations do not prove causation, and our results do not establish a positive or negative comprehensive university-wide return. End with the need to evaluate receipts, costs and the continuing funding commitment together.


## Backup only

### Alternative financial model

The school/year levels coefficient is 0.929, interval [0.821, 1.037], which includes full coverage. Keep this contrary sensitivity available. Primary annual and three-year change models remain below one after adjustment.

### Other pathways tested

Retain the complete primary application/enrollment and spending-success results, smaller-program interactions, Power Five/budget-size comparisons and exploratory basketball/AP findings in the technical reports. These analyses are outside the main story because their proposed advantages are unconfirmed or specification-sensitive. Insignificance does not prove no benefit.

### Qualified historical donation evidence

STE replication: +$191,243 athletic alumni operating gifts per extra football win, interval $62,114–$320,373; N=616/94 schools. Raw p=0.0041; Holm across ten outcomes=0.0412; across twenty tests=0.0742. Broader correction is inconclusive. All ten STE estimates match printed precision; IV remains partial. This is a separate earlier sample, historical source dollars and athletics-specific gifts. Do not combine it with the ticket coefficient.
