# Beyond the Scoreboard: Does University Investment in Athletics Pay Off?
AC 547 — Track A: Analyze

**What is the question/problem?** Universities devote substantial resources to athletics, but administrators need to know which benefits accompany that spending. We will examine whether higher athletic expenditures are associated with stronger overall competitive performance, whether success predicts subsequent athletics revenue, and whether it is followed by more university applications or first-time enrollment. We will also compare broad athletics success with success in high-profile football and men's basketball. Each link is a hypothesis to test, rather than an assumed financial payoff.

**Why is it important?** Athletics competes with academic programs and other university priorities for limited resources. An athletic department's reported revenue can include university support and student fees, so an apparently balanced budget does not necessarily indicate financial self-sufficiency. Athletic visibility might also create institutional benefits, but more applicants do not automatically produce additional students or tuition contribution. Administrators need evidence about these distinctions before using athletics as an investment or recruitment strategy.

**How will we address it?** We will use Python to combine public EADA athletics financial data, NACDA Learfield Directors' Cup standings, and NCES IPEDS admissions, enrollment, and financial data for 2010–2019. The registered Knight-Newhouse workbook has been obtained and staged; it will provide the preferred financial measures after source-definition and merge review. We will clean and join observations by university and year, report coverage and descriptive statistics, create charts, and estimate models that control for university and year differences. Robustness checks will examine timing, sample composition, scoring changes, and football/basketball measures. Finally, transparent accounting scenarios will show the enrollment contribution needed to offset hypothetical additional athletic costs, without interpreting observational associations as causal returns.
