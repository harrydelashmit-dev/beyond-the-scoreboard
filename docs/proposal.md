# Beyond the Scoreboard: Does University Investment in Athletics Pay Off?
AC 547 — Track A: Analyze

**What is the question/problem?** Does the financial return associated with university athletic investment depend on the type of competitive success? We will investigate three separate relationships: whether higher athletic spending predicts better overall or sport-specific performance; whether success predicts athletics ticket receipts, donations and revenue after allocated support; and whether success is followed by more applications or first-time enrollment through the Flutie Effect. We will compare Directors' Cup performance with football strength and nationally visible football and men's basketball achievements. Each link is a hypothesis, rather than an assumed financial payoff.

**Why is it important?** Athletics competes with academic programs and other university priorities for limited resources. An athletic department's reported revenue can include university support and student fees, so an apparently balanced budget does not necessarily indicate financial self-sufficiency. Athletic visibility might also create institutional benefits, but more applicants do not automatically produce additional students or tuition contribution. Administrators need evidence about these distinctions before using athletics as an investment or recruitment strategy.

**How will we address it?** We will use Python to combine registered Knight-Newhouse finances, EADA sport spending, NACDA standings, ESPN historical ratings/records/polls and NCES IPEDS admissions, enrollment and financial data for 2010–2019. The sources and school/year joins have been audited, and the initial feasibility analysis finds a consistent football–ticket association alongside uncertain spending and university-demand relationships. We will report descriptive statistics, charts, school and conference/year controls, school-clustered confidence intervals, model-specific samples and multiple-testing adjustments. Sensitivity checks examine timing, alternative success definitions, school growth, historical prominence and conference groups. Transparent break-even and NPV scenarios will distinguish applicants, first-time intake and enrolled student stock, without claiming that observational correlations identify causal financial returns.
